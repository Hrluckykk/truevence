BGV green, amber, red: what each status colour really means.

BGV reports use colour codes to signal the outcome of each check. Green means verified. Amber means attention needed. Red means adverse finding. But the line between amber and red is where most hiring mistakes happen. Here is exactly what each colour means and what action it requires.

July 2026 8 min read Truevence Editorial Team Filed under BGV Reporting
§ 01 — Why colour codes

Why BGV reports use colour codes.

Background verification reports can contain dozens of individual checks: identity, address, education, employment, criminal record, reference, and more. Reading through every row of raw data for every candidate is not practical for a hiring manager reviewing hundreds of files.

Colour codes compress that complexity into a single glance. Green tells you the check cleared. Amber tells you something needs attention. Red tells you there is an adverse finding. The system is designed to direct your attention, not to make the decision for you.

Green Verified. The source confirmed the information. No action needed.
Amber Attention needed. The check is incomplete, mismatched, or awaiting input. Not a rejection.
Red Adverse finding. The check confirmed a discrepancy, a false claim, or an adverse record.
The critical rule Amber is not red. Treating amber as red is the most common and most costly BGV mistake.

Green means go. Red means stop. Amber means slow down and look — not stop.

§ 02 — Green status

Green: verified.

A green status means the verification agency successfully confirmed the information with the primary source. The check is complete and the result is positive.

✓

What green means

The source confirmed the candidate's information. No discrepancy. No adverse record. The check is closed.

✓

What triggers it

Aadhaar matched, PAN matched, EPFO record confirmed, degree verified by the university, court search returned no records.

✓

What to do

Nothing. Green checks are clear. Move to the next step in your hiring process. No follow-up required.

Green is the outcome you want on every check. If every check on a report is green, the candidate has cleared background verification with no caveats. You can onboard with confidence.

§ 03 — Amber status

Amber: attention needed.

An amber status means the check could not be completed as submitted, but the verifier has not found an adverse result. It is a process state, not a verdict. Amber covers everything from "source did not respond" to "name mismatch" to "awaiting document."

!

What amber means

The check is incomplete or inconclusive. Not verified, but not disproven. The verifier needs something to proceed.

!

What triggers it

Unable to Verify, Insufficient documents, On Hold, source not responding, minor data mismatch, database coverage gap.

!

What to do

Request the missing document or correction, then re-run the check. Most amber statuses resolve to green on the second attempt.

Amber is not a rejection

Treating amber as red is the most expensive BGV mistake.

Amber is the most misread status in BGV. Hiring managers see anything that is not green and assume the worst. They reject the candidate. The candidate was never actually found to have a problem — the check simply was not completed.

The cost of misreading amber. You lose a qualified candidate, restart the hiring process, and pay recruitment costs again. If the candidate was rejected on an amber that would have resolved to green, you have also created potential legal exposure.

The correct response to amber. Request the missing information. Give the candidate a clear deadline. Re-run the check. Escalate to manual verification if the digital source cannot confirm. Only escalate to red if the re-run or manual check returns an adverse finding.

Amber buys you information. Red is a finding. Never confuse the two.

§ 04 — Red status

Red: adverse finding.

A red status means the verification produced a confirmed adverse result. The source did not merely fail to respond — it affirmatively returned information that contradicts what the candidate provided, or it returned a record that is disqualifying.

✕

What red means

The check confirmed a discrepancy, false claim, or adverse record. This is a finding, not a process state.

✕

What triggers it

Degree confirmed fake, employment dates falsified, criminal record found, identity document not belonging to the candidate.

✕

What to do

Follow your policy. Red typically triggers rejection or withdrawal of offer, but the decision should be documented and consistent.

  • Confirmed false education. The university states no such degree was awarded, or the degree belongs to a different person.
  • Falsified employment. The employer confirms the candidate never worked there, or the dates and designation are materially different from what was claimed.
  • Criminal record found. A court record search returns a conviction or pending case that the candidate did not disclose.
  • Identity fraud. The identity document does not belong to the candidate, or the candidate is not who they claim to be.
  • Material undisclosed adverse history. A record that the candidate was specifically required to disclose and did not.

Red does not automatically mean termination or rejection in every case. Your policy determines the consequence. But red is a confirmed finding, and it should be treated as a serious matter that requires a documented decision.

§ 06 — What determines colour

What makes a check go green, amber, or red.

The colour assigned to a check is not arbitrary. It follows from what the source returned and how the verifier interpreted it. Here is the logic.

1.

Source confirms.

The source returns a clear positive match. The name, dates, institution, and record all align with what the candidate provided. Result: green. No further action.

Clear match Source responsive No discrepancy
2.

Source cannot confirm.

The source is silent, the data does not match exactly, or the record is not in the accessible database. The verifier cannot say the information is false — only that it could not be confirmed. Result: amber.

No response Data mismatch Coverage gap
3.

Source contradicts.

The source affirmatively returns information that does not match what the candidate provided, or returns an adverse record. This is not a gap — it is a finding. Result: red.

Contradiction Adverse record Confirmed false

The key distinction is between "could not confirm" (amber) and "confirmed otherwise" (red). Most candidate frustration and most employer error comes from treating the first as the second.
