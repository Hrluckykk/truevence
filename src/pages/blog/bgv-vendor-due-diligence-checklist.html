BGV vendor due diligence: 32 points to verify before you sign.

A vendor who claims UIDAI authorisation, ISO certification, and 5-day turnaround on a sales slide is not the same as a vendor who can produce the documentation. Due diligence is the step that separates claims from evidence. This checklist gives you 32 specific points to verify, organised into seven categories, before you sign a background verification contract.

July 2026 10 min read Truevence Editorial Team Filed under Vendor Management
§ 01 — Why it matters

Why BGV vendor due diligence actually matters.

Every BGV vendor claims compliance, accuracy, and speed. Very few can produce documentation for all three on request. Due diligence is the process of asking for the evidence, verifying it independently where possible, and only then deciding whether to sign. Skipping it means inheriting whatever the vendor actually is, not what they appeared to be in the sales meeting.

The stakes are higher than most procurement processes. Under the DPDP Act, the employer remains the data fiduciary even when a vendor processes candidate data. A vendor whose compliance slips becomes your compliance problem. A vendor whose UIDAI authorisation lapses becomes your legal exposure.

Due diligence is what stops you from discovering in month six what you should have discovered in week two. The cost is a fortnight. The saving is a two-year mistake.

§ 02 — Legal and corporate

Legal and corporate verification (6 points).

The first category is straightforward. You are about to sign a contract with this entity, so you need to know that the entity actually exists, is in good standing, and has the legal right to do what they claim.

  • Certificate of incorporation with CIN verifiable on the MCA portal, not just a scanned PDF
  • GST registration with the GSTIN verifiable independently, and status showing as active
  • PAN and TAN matching the entity name, for tax and invoicing purposes
  • Registered office address confirmed against the MCA record, not a residential address
  • Directors and key management checked for disqualification lists or adverse media
  • No ongoing insolvency or winding-up proceedings under IBC or equivalent
§ 03 — Financial stability

Financial stability (5 points).

A BGV vendor that shuts down mid-contract leaves you with no verification capability overnight. Financial due diligence is not about checking that they are profitable. It is about checking that they will still be operating in 24 months.

Years · Revenue · Concentration

A vendor that depends on one client is a vendor that can disappear.

Years in operation. At least 3 years with continuity of leadership. Companies that have been around longer have survived at least one downturn and are statistically more likely to survive the next.

Audited financial statements for the last 2 years, or tax filings if audited statements are unavailable. Look for revenue stability rather than absolute size. A ₹5 crore business with stable revenue is safer than a ₹50 crore business with a 60 percent yearly swing.

Client concentration. Ask what percentage of revenue comes from the top three clients. Above 50 percent means a single contract loss could destabilise them. Above 70 percent is a serious risk signal.

Employee count and turnover. A BGV vendor is a people business. If they claim to run 500,000 checks a year with 30 employees, the numbers do not work. Ask for headcount in operations, verification, and field roles specifically.

Existing liabilities. Ask whether there are any outstanding tax disputes, legal claims, or regulatory actions. Vendors who have a clean record will provide this without hesitation.

§ 04 — Regulatory and compliance

Regulatory and compliance (6 points).

This is the category that requires the most evidence. Every claim must be backed by a document you can verify independently, not just a letter on their letterhead.

Claim Evidence to ask for How to verify
UIDAI AUA/ASA status Authorisation certificate with reference number Cross-check with UIDAI or via their published list
DPDP readiness Consent framework, retention policy, breach notification procedure Reviewed by your Legal team before signing
ISO 27001 Certificate with issuing body and expiry date Verify on the certification body's public register
SOC 2 Type II Latest report, at least the summary of findings Review exceptions and remediation notes
Professional indemnity Insurance certificate with coverage amount and expiry Contact the insurer directly if the amount is material
Cyber liability cover Certificate with breach response coverage defined Confirm the policy covers third-party data breaches
§ 05 — Operational capability

Operational capability (6 points).

Operational due diligence is where you verify that the vendor can actually do what they promised in the sales conversation. This is best done through a combination of documentation and a live walkthrough.

  • Field agent network with names of regional heads in at least the major cities you hire in, not just a claim of pan-India coverage
  • University relationship list showing which institutions they have direct verification paths with, versus which go through manual outreach
  • EPFO access confirmed, plus their process for cases where EPFO data is incomplete or missing
  • Court record coverage at the district level, not just high court and Supreme Court databases
  • Reference check methodology documented, including how many attempts they make before declaring a reference unreachable
  • Quality assurance process showing who reviews each report before it goes out, and how disputes are handled
§ 06 — Technology and security

Technology and security (5 points).

If the vendor handles candidate data through an API or a portal, technology due diligence is not optional. Even if your integration is minimal, they are still storing candidate records.

  • Data encryption at rest and in transit, with the specific standards documented (AES-256, TLS 1.2 or higher)
  • Access controls with role-based access, MFA, and audit logs on candidate data access
  • Penetration testing done at least annually, with the latest report available for review
  • Business continuity plan documented for source outages, API downtime, and regional disruption
  • Data residency confirmed as India-only, unless you have a specific business reason for cross-border processing
§ 07 — References

Reference and reputation (4 points).

References on a slide are marketing. References you actually speak to are evidence. Ask for two or three customers in your sector or of similar scale, and call them.

  • Two to three referenceable customers willing to take a live call, not just names on a deck
  • Reference questions answered specifically on turnaround, accuracy, escalation, and how the vendor behaves when something goes wrong
  • Adverse media check on the company and key management, done independently rather than relying on what the vendor tells you
  • Online reputation review including candidate reviews of the verification experience, where relevant
§ 08 — Red flags

Red flags that should stop the process.

Some findings during due diligence should end the conversation immediately, regardless of how attractive the pricing looks.

  • Cannot produce UIDAI authorisation certificate when they claim to run Aadhaar checks
  • Refuses to share a sample report with real structure, even redacted, before signing
  • No DPDP documentation or vague answers about consent capture and data retention
  • ISO certificate that does not verify on the issuing body's public register
  • Financial statements that do not reconcile or repeated requests to avoid the topic
  • Reference customers who cannot be reached despite repeated attempts
  • Legal disputes with previous clients that the vendor dismisses without explanation
  • Pressure to sign quickly with a "special pricing" that expires in days

Any one of these alone might be explained. Two or more together almost always means the vendor has something to hide.
